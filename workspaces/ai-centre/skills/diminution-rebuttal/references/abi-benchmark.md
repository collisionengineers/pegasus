# ABI 20% Benchmark — The Strongest Standalone Point

Include this argument in every rebuttal. It survives cross-examination because both the ABI guidance and the EVA scale's outputs are matters of record.

---

## The argument

Use this reference for the ABI/Cat S inversion and for EVA formula proportionality defects. Do not
present the arithmetic as a universal market rule; present it as a stress test showing the formula
can produce results no real market hierarchy would support.

**Step 1 — Establish the ABI benchmark.**
ABI guidance is that vehicles carrying a previous total loss marker (Cat N or Cat S) typically attract a deduction of around **20%** from open-market value.

**Step 2 — Define what a Cat S vehicle is.**
A Cat S vehicle carries:
- A **permanent HPI database marker** visible to any buyer conducting a history check
- A recorded determination that the vehicle sustained **structural damage**
- A recorded insurer determination that the vehicle was **uneconomic to repair**

A buyer cannot acquire a Cat S vehicle without knowing its history. The deduction compensates for permanent, recorded, structural damage.

**Step 3 — Show the EVA formula exceeds this benchmark.**
The EVA scale runs: 3.5% / 6.5% / 15% / 22% (base stigma bands). The market multiplier runs: ×1.25 / ×1.15 / ×1.0 / ×0.9 / ×0.8.

Maximum theoretical EVA output: **22% × 1.25 = 27.5%**.

This is achievable in practice. On non-fault side claims, repair authorisation commonly runs to 75% (and occasionally 80%) of PAV — which places those cases in the upper bands of the EVA scale.

**Step 4 — State the inversion.**
The EVA methodology, at its upper bands, is capable of producing a greater deduction on a properly repaired vehicle **with no database marker and no structural compromise** than the ABI guidance for a formally recorded Cat S write-off.

That cannot be right.

**Step 5 — Extend downward.**
A scale that breaks down at its upper end cannot be relied upon at its lower end either. Even where the present figure is modest, it is produced by the same methodology that generates inconsistent results in heavier cases. The unreliability of the instrument is not cured by the fact that a particular output happens to be a small number.

## Repair-cost proportionality defect (loss greater than the repair cost)

Use this where the repair was small relative to PAV. It is exactly calculable, so run the numbers —
a worked figure lands far harder than an assertion.

**The mechanism:** the base stigma percentage never falls below 3.5%. For any repair under 20% of
PAV the formula applies that flat floor however small the repair, so it does not scale down as the
repair gets smaller. A sufficiently minor repair therefore produces a claimed "loss" larger than
the repair itself cost.

**The exact test.** Expressed as percentages of PAV, the claimed loss equals or exceeds the repair
cost wherever the repair cost is at or below the threshold for the multiplier applied:

| Market multiplier | Claimed loss ≥ repair cost where repair ≤ |
|---|---|
| 1.25 (≤5 comparables) | 4.38% of PAV |
| 1.15 (6–10) | 4.03% of PAV |
| 1.0 (11–20) | 3.5% of PAV |
| 0.9 (21–30) | 3.15% of PAV |
| 0.8 (>30) | 2.8% of PAV |

**Worked illustration** (PAV £15,000): a £400 cosmetic repair (2.67% of PAV) at multiplier 1.25
produces 4.375% — a claimed diminution of £656, some £256 more than the repair cost. A £100 repair
at multiplier 0.8 produces £420 — over four times the repair cost. The smaller the repair, the
wider the gap, because the 3.5% floor never falls.

**The conclusion to draw:** a methodology that values the loss caused by a repair at more than the
repair itself cost is not measuring anything real. No buyer treats a £400 cosmetic repair as having
stripped £650 from a £15,000 car. If using the point, state three numbers in the draft: repair
cost, claimed diminution, and claimed diminution as a percentage of repair cost.

Both defects — this one and the 20% inversion above — flow from the same source: fixed percentages
applied mechanically from repair cost and comparable count alone, with no account of the nature of
the damage or any observed market effect. A scale unreliable at both ends is not reliable in the
middle either.

## Two-car thought experiment (use this — it lands harder)

> Consider two vehicles of identical make, model, year, and specification. Vehicle A has been repaired following a collision — bolt-on panels replaced, metallic refinish, no structural work, no HPI marker, no detectable record of the repair on any database a buyer would consult. Vehicle B has been written off following a more serious collision — structural damage, uneconomic to repair, permanent Cat S marker, visible to any buyer conducting a history check.
>
> The ABI guidance values Vehicle B at approximately 20% below market. The EVA formula, applied to Vehicle A in a case where repair costs represent, say, 75% of PAV, produces a deduction of 27.5%. On EVA's own methodology, the buyer of a properly repaired, unmarked, structurally sound vehicle should pay more than the buyer of a recorded Cat S write-off. That cannot be right.

---

## Presentation note

Use "that cannot be right" — it is stronger than abstract language. One clear statement of the inversion, backed by the specific figures (ABI ~20%, EVA max 27.5%), is sufficient. Do not repeat the argument three times or stack caveats.
